# The house that kept the wrong account
## Author-facing story bible: spoilers

Original proposed fiction. Only Chapter I is implemented. Later acts below are a complete narrative direction and evidence specification, not playable or formally solver-verified content. Do not silently rewrite Bellweather, Briar House or other existing Alibi casebooks to fit it.

## The emotional centre

A house built to preserve knowledge failed to preserve an honest account of one person's life. Its curator, Mara Vale, died during a flood in 1911. The official version remembers her as someone who went back for valuable objects and blames the clockmaker Rowan Finch for arriving too late to help. Neither claim deserves the confidence with which it was printed.

The player does not undo the death, meet a literal ghost or discover that every sad event was a calculated murder. They recover a more accurate account of a preventable failure, a brave but costly response and the institutional choices that made an easy scapegoat useful. The house changes because its account changes.

The central question develops from “Was Finch late?” into “What was the warning meant to protect?” and finally “What should this place preserve now?” The last question is a creative choice, not an objectively scored moral answer.

## Characters

**Mara Vale, curator.** Interested in how ordinary people use patterns, not merely in rare objects. She had been collecting village games, bridge puzzles, domestic calculating tools and children's handmade mechanisms for an exhibition called Questions We Share. She is remembered through specific habits: pencilled alternatives on labels, a repaired game piece kept beside a perfect one, correspondence asking a maker how a toy was actually used. Avoid turning her into a saint whose only function is to die.

**Rowan Finch, clockmaker and instrument maintainer.** Precise about mechanisms, less skilled at arguing with the people who pay for their care. His maintenance note admits that an adjustment was deferred. That is evidence of a fault, not proof that he caused the entire disaster. Later evidence clears the timing accusation and establishes where he acted, while leaving room to examine his own reluctance to challenge the trustees.

**Edmund Wren, chair of the estate trustees.** Approved postponing an expensive relief-gate repair and a temporary warning-system arrangement during reopening work. His fear was financial collapse and the closure of a public collection he valued. After the flood, he accepted and edited an account that protected the institution. His motives explain the concealment; they do not make it harmless. Do not make him a theatrical murderer or give him a confession that replaces all deduction.

**Iona Bell, housekeeper and keeper of service records.** Preserves practical records that the public archive overlooks. Her notes are fragmentary and sometimes uncertain. She is not an infallible narrator. She distinguishes what she heard from what she inferred, providing a model for the player.

**Ada Vale, Mara's adult sister.** Kept correspondence about the planned exhibition and later memorial. She wanted Mara's work remembered without turning her death into a spectacle. She is the emotional counterweight to the institution's public plaque.

**The present-day keeper.** Invites the player to catalogue the reopening collection. They are not secretly omniscient. The invitation authorises investigation and careful labelling, not trespass into unrelated personal accounts. Their understated responses let the player lead the inquiry.

## The established truth for future authors

The station clock was seventeen minutes fast on the relevant night. A ticket marked 21:17 therefore places Finch's departure at 21:00 under the case's explicit documentary assumptions. A seven-minute footpath made arrival before the observatory's 21:10 bell possible. The first chapter proves only that possibility.

In the proposed full account, Finch actually reached the tower before the calibrated bell, then went to the relief-gate mechanism. Mara used the service stair to go towards the river and warn the village. The warning network's temporary selector arrangement did not reach the village branch. A previously deferred repair had left the relief gate unreliable. These are distinct failures; a single corrected clock does not explain either one.

Mara reached the village warning point. People began to evacuate. She died when the old river crossing failed during her return. The framed official account later used the uncorrected ticket, omitted the service stair, and described her journey as an attempt to rescue the collection. The omissions support a convenient story about individual negligence rather than shared decisions.

What the final evidence does not establish: Mara's exact final thoughts, every private motive, or a counterfactual guarantee that one different action would have saved her. These limits should survive the ending.

## Chapter I: The Seventeenth Minute

**Playable now.** The invitation asks the player to put the house in order. A three-wheel constraint lock offers a quick success. In the Long Library, an ordering puzzle releases a maintenance slip. The Observatory asks the player to correct the station time. The Map Room asks for a feasible route before the calibrated bell. The Keeper's Study asks what the two results jointly justify.

The tempting wrong answer is not another arithmetic mistake. It is the claim that a possible journey proves Finch made it. The correct answer rejects the accusation's timing argument while leaving his actual whereabouts unresolved. A cabinet latch opens the Unrecorded Stair. An unsigned margin claims Mara went to warn the village, not save the collection. It is labelled unverified testimony.

Fairness: every needed number is stated in the relevant board, the route times are explicit case stipulations, no real astronomical knowledge is required, and no secret is obtainable only from video or sound. The museum's Bridges or Lo Shu activity supplies an alternate Map Room access path. The main chain remains finite and reset-free.

Emotional beat: the first visible progress is an act of restraint. The player learns not to accuse or absolve more confidently than the records allow.

## Chapter II: The Room Between the Rooms

**Planned.** The player compares the 1911 survey, later official plan and service circulation. A short spatial puzzle establishes that a route connects the study stair to the working courtyard. The Dining Room and Portrait Gallery provide two perspectives on who was visible in the public record. A guest list and a service list are different documents; a missing name in one does not imply absence from the house.

Quest sequence: inspect the stair; learn adjacency comparison in the museum; align or list plan differences; locate the service courtyard; reconcile two witnesses who used different room names; identify who could independently verify Mara's direction of travel.

Payoff: the player can corroborate the direction of her journey using more than the unsigned note. They discover that the omission in the published plan was made after the flood. That establishes alteration, not yet motive.

Optional secret: a workshop draughting mark appears on replaced plaques in several rooms. Recognising it reveals an archive drawer of discarded captions. It supplies character and an alternative hint, never the only required witness.

Required author test: enumerate feasible routes under both plans and prove that the selected adjacency difference is unique. Check alternate terminology explicitly. Do not accept visual overlay resemblance as the sole solution rule.

## Chapter III: What the Warning Reached

**Planned.** The Rookery, Clockmaker's Workshop, Cistern Vaults and Flooded Archive form a systems investigation. A signal-path puzzle shows the actual temporary connection, not the intended diagram. A bounded water-level or valve puzzle clarifies which volumes and connections matter. A work order and an invoice establish that an unreliable mechanism was known before the disaster.

The philosophical turn is from blaming a person who touched a system to reconstructing how the system behaved. Separate intended configuration, installed configuration and observed result. Do not simulate real flooding with invented precise physics while presenting it as an engineering fact; author a small, explicit game model.

Payoff: the village did not receive the house's automatic warning through the temporary circuit. The relief gate was a separate failure. The player can now explain why Mara might need to travel personally even while a bell was audible in the castle.

Optional secret: an instrument with two labels, one for its inventor and one for its maker, opens a museum attribution quest. This ties the institutional theme to how exhibits are credited without using a lecture about the plot.

Required author test: truth tables for circuit state, bounded water solver, deliberate ambiguous and impossible fixtures, no prerequisite cycle between the archive record and the mechanism that reveals it.

## Chapter IV: The Other Bank

**Planned.** The Seven-Arch Bridge, Boathouse, orchard path and Court of Testimony establish a sequence of rescue actions. Boat capacity and movement constraints are a bounded scheduling problem. Independent records should corroborate Mara's warning and Finch's actual presence before the bell. The exact return-crossing interval can remain uncertain.

The player must reject an attractive but unsupported reconstruction, then select the chronology that satisfies all established constraints. Give each witness a perspective, not an arbitrary “always lies” label. A witness can be mistaken about a clock without inventing everything they report.

Payoff: Mara's journey was towards people. Finch's early arrival is now established by later evidence, not smuggled into the first chapter. The flood remains tragic; discovering the truth does not create a miraculous alternate ending.

Required author test: enumerate all chronologies under the stated constraints; identify what is unique and what remains an interval. If several details remain possible, the final answer must ask for the shared conclusion rather than one author's preferred complete ordering.

## Chapter V: An Honest Exhibition

**Planned.** The player finds the report draft and its published version. The evidence supports deliberate omission and institutional self-protection. A final synthesis board asks the player to connect public claims to supporting or contradicting records, with an explicit “not established” category. A private letter adds emotional understanding only after the factual reconstruction; it must not serve as a confession that solves all mechanics.

The closing activity is curatorial. Replace the old plaque with a supported account. Choose which objects to exhibit, where a memorial garden should sit in the authored grounds, and what name a new public room should carry from an authored selection. These creative choices do not alter the historical facts or earn a superior morality score.

The ending opens a museum room dedicated to Questions We Share. Future personal-garden and model-village keepsakes can reference the reopening. The story ends, but the place remains useful.

A proposed final line: “The house did not need a perfect account. It needed one that could be corrected.” Use sparingly; the environmental changes should do most of the emotional work.

## Evidence and spoiler policy

Keep an author-only ledger of propositions, prerequisites, sources, alternatives and permitted conclusions. Player-facing clues use short, natural language. Historical museum truth and fictional estate truth occupy separate registries. A badge description, asset filename, video poster or future-room card must not reveal a solution ahead of its intended gate in production.

Hints progress from orientation, to relevant constraint, to method, to an explicitly labelled worked solution. A player may request the answer without sacrificing the main narrative. Recovered records remain available in the notebook. Required evidence never disappears when a location is restored.

Do not make a player's settings, backup controls, real files, current email or personal history part of an unannounced puzzle. The game can challenge its fictional catalogue without making the actual interface untrustworthy.
