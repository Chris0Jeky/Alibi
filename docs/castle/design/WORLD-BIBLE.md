# Alibi: Wrenmere Castle
## A house of unfinished questions

Design proposal, 9 September 2026. Wrenmere is a working name, not an approved rename of Alibi. The accompanying build is a separate playable first chapter. It does not replace the existing site or its saves.

## The proposition

Alibi becomes a place that teaches you to notice, reason, revise and remember. A puzzle is not merely a unit of content in a list. It is something left on a table, a method recovered from another century, a disagreement between records, or a pattern built into the house.

The player is invited to catalogue a countryside castle before its public reopening. Its collection includes recreational mathematics, mechanical toys, games, astronomical instruments and local records. While arranging the collection, the player discovers that the house's official history is also an arrangement, and not an innocent one.

The three parts should reinforce one another: the puzzle cabinet supplies depth, the museum supplies context and transferable methods, and the castle supplies continuity and emotional meaning. The Quiet Wing lets discovery turn into reflection and personal creation. The aim is not to put an elaborate map in front of a catalogue. It is to make learning a method change what the player can see in the world.

Keep “A little room to think” as Alibi's intimate promise. Use “A house of unfinished questions” for the castle campaign. Neither promises intelligence gains or turns the game into a test of personal worth.

## Six fundamentals, expressed through play

**A record is not its explanation.** The opening separates the ticket, the clock correction and the journey hypothesis. The notebook labels record, derived fact, model and unverified testimony differently. Later chapters repeat the distinction with maps, letters and testimony, not by repeating a lecture.

**A useful answer may be a limit.** The bridge exhibit rewards an impossibility proof. The player should occasionally be right to say “this cannot be done under these rules” or “there is not enough information.” Such tasks need explicit feedback so an impossible instance is never confused with broken content.

**Changing representation changes the problem.** A walk becomes a graph; a series of objects becomes an order relation; an instrument reading becomes an equation; a building plan becomes an adjacency model. The museum gives the player a lens that can be used later, not just a fact to memorise.

**Revision is progress.** Undo, a corrected notebook connection and a rejected overclaim should feel normal. A player can change a theory without losing collectibles, affection or access. A journal may preserve an optional local history of changed hypotheses, but it must not broadcast private notes.

**Play belongs to many histories.** The museum distinguishes an original object, later accounts, modern reconstructions and adaptations. A picturesque legend is not silently promoted to an invention date. The castle is the exhibition's setting, not the fictional birthplace of every puzzle tradition.

**Understanding should leave something worth caring for.** Solving the house can lead to planting, making, curating and companionship. These are not chores required to keep a streak alive. The ending should make the Quiet Wing more meaningful, rather than make the whole app feel finished and disposable.

## A persistent place, not a daily reset

Blue Prince's official description uses drafted rooms and a floor plan that resets daily. This design deliberately chooses a different structure: a stable estate with new interpretations, repairs, shortcuts and archive layers. Familiarity becomes useful. A player returning after a month still knows where the library is.

Borrow the pleasure of layered discovery, not another game's floor plan, room names, numbered destination, plot or movement economy. Wrenmere should have its own spatial logic: old masonry, later additions, service corridors, garden paths and a river that explains why some routes were built.

There are three navigation scales. The **estate** shows the castle, grounds and outbuildings. The **room** presents one readable composition with inspectable objects and a clear route back. The **object** becomes the puzzle or exhibit. The player can also use a searchable room directory and the ordinary puzzle catalogue. Atmospheric navigation is an option, not a tax on returning to a Sudoku.

In the prototype, room interiors and an estate map work. The archive lens switches between today's view and an illustrated 1911 survey layer. It is not a simulation of time travel. Production can later add documented era layers to museum exhibits and authored fictional layers to the castle; these two forms of history must remain labelled.

## The player experience

A first visit should offer an invitation, a plainly visible small puzzle and an unrestricted museum door. The player need not read the tragic premise before trying a question. “Follow the castle story” can be turned off. That means a quieter visit, not an inaccessible set of puzzles.

A returning visit should remember a useful destination, the last unfinished board and the last unresolved question. Keep “continue puzzle” one action away from the front door. Do not play a cinematic each launch. The museum can suggest a small exhibit related to the player's last puzzle family, but never require it to resume a saved game.

A productive session might be: enter the library, finish one ordering puzzle, discover a maintenance slip, correct a clock upstairs, reconsider a route, then spend two minutes in the museum learning why another route is impossible. The user stops because they are satisfied, not because a timer says a plant must be harvested.

A short session might be only petting a familiar companion, swapping a few Lo Shu tiles, or arranging a postcard. It should still feel like visiting Alibi.

## Room design grammar

Every production room needs a reason to exist beyond a colour scheme. Author a short identity sentence, a characteristic material/light/sound, a family or curated theme, one method to practise, a visible local question, a longer secret, at least one connection to another location, and an accessible alternative to each decorative interaction.

Rooms can contain more than one family. The Long Library should curate deduction and ordering around a bookish theme, not mechanically accept every puzzle whose engine is called dossier. Conversely, a single family can appear in several settings with different narrative roles. A water-level board in the kitchen teaches conservation; a harder water-level board in the cistern tests the same idea in the flood investigation.

The world is deliberately wider than the first implementation. The directory marks future locations as planned, not as doors a determined player can grind open. In a shipped campaign, unrevealed secrets should generally remain silhouettes or be absent from the directory; the prototype exposes its design catalogue so the owner can review scope.

## Make the rooms feel different

The upper castle is quiet, exposed and cool. Its instruments are brass and its sky is large; a small movement across a star chart should feel deliberate. The social rooms have warmer upholstery and uncomfortable absences: an unlabelled portrait, an extra place setting, a statement preserved more carefully than another. The working rooms are tactile and practical: valves, stamps, recipes, bell wires and service plans. They tell the history from the perspective of people who kept the estate functioning.

The gardens offer breathing space and changes of scale. A hedge seen from inside is a navigation problem; from the belvedere it is a drawing. The orchard places living growth beside old geometry. Below ground, the same architectural motifs become harder to trust: a waterline cuts across the official floor plan, a pipe passes through an allegedly solid wall, a discarded plan is more useful than the framed one upstairs.

The Quiet Wing is warm without being sugary. It should feel cared for, not aggressively cheerful. Its museum admits uncertainty. Its conservatory offers a chair facing the garden rather than the clock. Its future companions have habits and favourite places, not urgent needs. The Model Village lets players make a personal response to what they have learned.

## Interaction direction

Use a short threshold transition on deliberate entry, with a static replacement under reduced motion. Keep the puzzle board stable once the player starts thinking. Do not leave animated dust, parallax, camera drift or moving background video behind dense clues.

Completion has three layers: the answer is acknowledged, the relevant record or change is shown, and the next meaningful choice appears. A small latch sound, a light coming on, a corrected caption or an opened shortcut is more memorable than spraying points over the screen. Sound is off until chosen. A text status always conveys the same result. Haptics are an optional later integration, never the sole feedback channel.

Restoration must not obscure unfinished play. If a room changes lighting after completion, maintain readable contrast and the selected control. Keep a visible control to inspect what changed. Use tactile metaphors sparingly: turning a dial is enjoyable once; repeating a long unlocking animation across twenty boards is friction.

No precision pixel hunts. Important hotspots should have generous targets, keyboard access, readable names and an “objects in this room” list. Optional purely decorative secrets may be subtle, but they cannot gate required evidence without a progressive hint path.

## The castle itself as a puzzle

A platform-wide mystery can be built from five authored systems rather than arbitrary secret buttons:

1. **Contradictory surveys.** Align two plans to reveal a missing connection. The accessible form is a list of adjacency differences, not an alt-text spoiler or a pixel-perfect drag.
2. **A language of repairs.** Tool marks, changed plaques and repeated replacement materials identify which objects were altered together. Teach that visual vocabulary in the workshop first.
3. **A provenance trail.** A museum label names a donor, a letter explains a date, and a service ledger challenges the attribution. The catalogue becomes evidence, but settings, privacy and backup controls never become fictional traps.
4. **Knowledge shortcuts.** Understanding parity, symmetry or encoding opens an optional route. Players may still use a slower, explicit sequence when the clever observation is missed.
5. **The final exhibition.** Players reconstruct the public account from supported claims and sources. The last act repairs the house's explanation of itself.

Every major secret needs an authoring record: prerequisite knowledge, discovery cue, solution rule, alternate access, source of confirmation, resulting state changes and a spoiler-safe hint ladder. Automated reachability must rule out dependency cycles. Human testing must assess whether players understand why an inference follows.

## The Quiet Wing's new purpose

Give it four recurring jobs. It is the museum where methods gain history; the reading room where the notebook becomes understandable; the conservatory where discoveries become personal compositions; and the companion hearth where returning has a small social texture. The existing garden, realm builder, calm games and field notes are investments to preserve, not placeholders to discard.

The museum should have “handle, notice, learn, transfer” as its rhythm. Try the object first. Offer a small observation. Explain the relevant history and uncertainty. Then point to another place where the method helps. The player chooses whether to follow that connection.

Later curatorial quests can ask the player to arrange three objects around a common idea, correct an overconfident label, or compare two reconstructed rulesets. A good museum reward is a new question the player now understands how to ask. Cosmetic keepsakes are secondary.

## What this pass actually supplies

The independent HTML build implements ten visitable locations, ten puzzle/deduction activities, a first-chapter secret, three sourced exhibit experiences, illustrated estate and room views, a two-era visual lens, hints and explicit worked solutions, a notebook, local/session save handling, and an optional original film. Twenty-two additional locations are reviewable design entries.

It does not integrate the 324-puzzle catalogue, implement Chapters II–V, deliver companion XP, change garden rewards, expand the city, provide multiplayer, or deploy the castle into current Alibi. Those boundaries are intentional and visible. Integration and the deferred systems have their own plans and acceptance gates.

## Success criteria before expanding

Watch a small group of actual players attempt the opening without guidance. Record whether they can find the museum, recover from a wrong answer, explain why the time correction matters, distinguish possibility from proof and return to a desired puzzle without feeling lost. Ask what they remember about the house, not just whether they liked the colours.

Treat these as questions to measure, not already-achieved metrics. The automated checks establish mechanics, not emotional impact, educational transfer or human difficulty. A polished six-room loop that players remember is a better foundation than thirty-two rooms with indistinguishable menus.
