# The Museum of Questions

## A time machine made of methods

Give each exhibit four layers: an object or problem to handle, a short historical context, an explicit boundary around what is known, and a transferable method. The player can stop at any layer. A room should not require reading a long essay before a piece can be moved.

The time-machine experience should let a player move between representations: a surviving object, a documented account, a modern reconstruction and a new question built from it. These are not interchangeable. Use visible labels such as “documented object”, “legend”, “modern model”, “original adaptation” and “castle fiction”.

Where an era changes the rules, the change must be stated and reversible. Do not imply that a modern puzzle interface reproduces ancient play simply because its background looks old. Where history is uncertain, make the uncertainty part of the interaction: compare two reconstructions, identify which rule was assumed, or ask what evidence would distinguish them.

A first expansion should choose a few well-supported exhibits across regions and eras rather than claim a complete universal history. Keep a slot for objects or traditions not yet represented. Consult specialist sources before interpreting sacred or culturally specific material as a generic toy.

## Curatorial gameplay

“Correct the label” can show three short candidate claims and let the player identify the one the evidence supports. “Two reconstructions” can let the player switch one rule and observe a changed strategy. “Build an exhibition” can ask the player to group three objects by a method, with several defensible arrangements. “What is missing?” can distinguish missing evidence from proof of absence.

These activities should not be graded like vocabulary tests. Give specific feedback about a reason, not a generic red cross. Permit multiple valid classifications when the prompt allows them. A player can explain an alternative in a private note without an LLM declaring their personality or intelligence.

## Editorial acceptance

Every published exhibit needs a source record, content author, checked date, adaptation boundary, correct solution/termination rule and rights record for each asset. Have someone other than the author review the clue wording. Exhaustive checking is appropriate for small spaces; larger tasks need bounded solver evidence, deliberate negative fixtures and human review.

Source links remain available but are not required for offline play. Never require an external video or a current live webpage to solve a permanent exhibit. If a source disappears, preserve the citation metadata and a clear editorial note; do not invent a substitute historical fact.
